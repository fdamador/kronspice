## Design (`solver/sparse.go`)

- **Algorithm.** Gilbert–Peierls left-looking LU (SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput.,
  1988): column k of L and U comes from a sparse triangular solve whose
  nonzero pattern is the set reachable from A(:,k) in the graph of L,
  found by a non-recursive depth-first search (Davis, *Direct Methods for
  Sparse Linear Systems*, 2006, `cs_reach`/`cs_lu`, reimplemented from the
  text). Work is proportional to the arithmetic.
- **Ordering.** Exact minimum degree on the pattern of A + Aᵀ (ties to the
  lowest index, so it is deterministic), computed once per pattern.
- **Pivoting.** Partial pivoting with diagonal preference (KLU, Davis &
  Palamadai Natarajan 2010): the diagonal is kept when it is at least
  10⁻³ of the column's largest entry, which keeps the fill-reducing order
  where that is numerically safe. Branch rows of voltage sources (zero
  diagonal) pivot off the diagonal as needed.
- **Singularity.** Same criterion as the dense backend (pivot below
  n·10⁻¹⁴ of the largest entry), so evolved circuits get the same error
  class on either backend.
- **Pattern discovery.** Devices stamp through the same `Add(i, j, v)`; the
  first stamp of an entry creates its slot, later ones reuse it. Because
  devices stamp in the same order every iteration, the k-th stamp after
  `Clear` is matched against the k-th stamp of the previous pass before
  falling back to a map lookup. A device that stamps a new entry later
  (pattern growth) triggers a new ordering at the next solve.
- **Generic core.** One implementation serves `Sparse` (float64, DC and
  transient) and `CSparse` (complex128, AC).

## Backend selection

`solver.Auto` (the default) uses the dense LU below 30 unknowns and the
sparse LU from 30 up (`solver.DenseThreshold`), as CLAUDE.md §3 asks; most
evolved circuits are small and stay dense. Force one with
`analysis.Options{Backend: solver.DenseLU}` / `solver.SparseLU` (also inside
`TranOptions` and the GA's `AnalysisOptions`), or `kronspice -solver
dense|sparse|auto` on the command line. `Sim.Backend()` reports the
choice.

## Benchmarks (2 vCPU container, Go 1.x)

Solve only, restamp + factor + solve of a resistor grid with two voltage
sources (`go test -bench GridSolve ./solver`):

| Unknowns | Dense | Sparse | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 | 5.5 µs | 4.5 µs | 1.2× |
| 66 | 30.6 µs | 19.2 µs | 1.6× |
| 258 | 577 µs | 170 µs | 3.4× |
| 1026 | 16.3 ms | 1.54 ms | 10.6× |

Whole transient, 1 µs of an RC mesh with diode clamps and a pulse source
(`go test -bench MeshTran ./analysis`):

| Nodes | Dense | Sparse | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | 0.88 ms | 1.09 ms | 0.81× (dense wins) |
| 64 | 6.4 ms | 4.4 ms | 1.46× |
| 256 | 91 ms | 37 ms | 2.5× |
| 576 | 640 ms | 114 ms | 5.6× |

For the whole transient, sparse overtakes dense between 16 and 64 nodes,
consistent with the threshold of 30 unknowns. Fill on a 40 × 40 grid
(1604 unknowns): A has 7,848 entries, L 19,880, U 21,493.

## Not done here (later)

- Symbolic reuse across factorizations (refactor with a fixed pivot
  sequence, as KLU's `klu_refactor`): the Gilbert–Peierls search already
  costs only as much as the arithmetic, so this is an optimization for
  later, not a requirement of M6.
- Approximate minimum degree (AMD) for very large circuits; the exact
  minimum degree used here runs once per pattern and is fast enough at
  the sizes the GA produces.
- `github.com/rwl/lufact` (§5) was not needed: a stdlib-only
  implementation keeps the core free of dependencies and the build
  offline.
